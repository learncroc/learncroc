# Why we forget

Ebbinghaus spent years memorising lists of nonsense syllables and testing himself at different delays. He found that most of what he learned faded within the first day, and that the loss slowed down after that. The shape he drew is now called the forgetting curve: a steep drop at first, then a long, shallow tail.

The curve is not fixed. Material that means something to you fades more slowly than nonsense syllables, and material you have already reviewed fades more slowly than material you saw once. Each successful review seems to flatten the curve, so the next drop takes longer to arrive.

# Spacing beats cramming

If you study the same thing for an hour, it matters how you split that hour. Several short sessions spread over days lead to better recall weeks later than one long session the night before. Researchers call this the spacing effect, and it has been found with words, facts, motor skills and even in other animals.

Cramming can feel more effective, because right after a long session the material is fresh and easy to recall. That ease is misleading. It measures how accessible the memory is now, not how durable it will be next month.

# Retrieval is the workout

Rereading a page makes it feel familiar, but familiarity is not the same as being able to produce the answer. Trying to recall something, even when you fail, strengthens the memory more than looking at it again. This is the testing effect, and it is one reason flashcards work when you answer before you flip them.

A failed attempt followed by the correct answer is especially useful. The error marks the spot where your knowledge was wrong, and the correction lands harder because you just committed to something else. Lots of people assume errors are wasted effort, but here they do real work.

# Scheduling the reviews

Spaced repetition software puts these ideas together. Each card gets a due date. When you recall a card successfully, the next interval gets longer; when you fail, it shrinks again. The goal is to review each card just before you would have forgotten it, when recall is hard but still possible.

Modern schedulers estimate two numbers per card: how stable the memory is and how likely you are to recall it today. They pick the review day where the predicted chance of recall drops to a target, often around ninety percent. Raising the target means more reviews and fewer lapses; lowering it means fewer reviews and more forgetting.

# Mixing it up

When you practise several kinds of problem, interleaving them works better than finishing one kind before starting the next. Interleaving forces you to decide which method a problem needs, which blocked practice never asks you to do. It feels harder and slower while you do it, and that difficulty is part of why it works.
